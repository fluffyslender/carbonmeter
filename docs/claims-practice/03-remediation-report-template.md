## 2. What changed, briefly

The **Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive (EU) 2024/825** became applicable across the EU on **27 September 2026**. It amends the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive by adding practices to a blacklist. Blacklisted practices are prohibited outright: there is no case-by-case assessment and **no substantiation defence** — a footnote does not rescue a banned headline.

The four prohibitions relevant to this review:

1. Claiming **neutral, reduced or positive environmental impact on the basis of offsetting** — expressly regardless of the quality or certification standard of the credits purchased.
2. **Generic environmental claims** without demonstrated recognised excellence.
3. Displaying a **sustainability label** not based on a certification scheme or established by a public authority — including self-designed badges.
4. **Unsubstantiated claims about future environmental performance.**

**What remains fully permitted**, and is the basis of every replacement we recommend: claims grounded in **actual emission reductions in your own operations and value chain**, stated with their method and evidence.

**A note on what this is not based on.** The separate *Green Claims Directive* proposal is frequently cited as a forthcoming obligation. The Commission announced its intention to withdraw it in June 2025 and it has not proceeded. If an adviser or vendor has told you to prepare for it, they are working from outdated material. Nothing in this report depends on it.

### Exposure

Penalties are set by each Member State. For widespread cross-border infringements, maximum available fines must reach **at least 4% of annual turnover**, or at least €2 million where turnover figures are unavailable. Penalty criteria expressly include the **duration** of the infringement — so how long a claim stays live is itself an aggravating factor, which is why Section 4 is ordered by urgency rather than by page.

### Which rules actually reach you

The Directive amends consumer-protection law, so it governs **business-to-consumer** practices; pure business-to-business practices fall outside its scope at EU level. We are not going to tell you the blacklist directly governs a sentence in a tender document.

**We nonetheless recommend holding all public material to the consumer standard**, for four practical reasons: you cannot segment a website by audience, and a public page is reachable by consumers whatever you intended; business-to-business misleading advertising is separately regulated and gives competitors a cause of action; your enterprise customers impose the standard contractually because their own reporting depends on yours; and maintaining two standards fails in practice, because copy migrates from a deck to a page to an ad.

### Record what you change, and when

For each change, keep: the original wording, the date first published, the date changed, the new wording, and who approved it. A single spreadsheet is sufficient.

This matters as much as the fix. Prompt, documented, self-initiated correction is a materially stronger position than a change nobody can date — and **if you cannot show when you fixed something, you cannot rebut an assumption that it was live throughout.**

### Closing the gate

Remediation is worth doing once. To avoid repeating it, add to your content workflow:

1. **The five rules.** Never net a payment against a figure. Never express a contribution in tonnes. Claim an action, not a state. Never state a figure without its method. Never display a self-made badge.
2. **A named owner** for environmental claims, with authority to refuse copy.
3. **One approval step** before any environmental claim publishes, and always before paid advertising.
4. **Approved wording for employees to copy** — people post about company events regardless, and a prohibition produces risk from everyone who does not read it.

## 7. Where to take legal advice

This report helps you draft accurately. Obtain qualified legal advice before:

- publishing any environmental claim in **paid advertising**
- making any claim **comparing yourself to a named competitor**
- relying on a **third-party certification or scheme logo**
- making any claim in a **prospectus, fund document or public tender**
- responding to a **challenge or query from any authority, competitor or customer**
- passing on a **supplier's neutrality claim** as your own

On the last point: reselling a supplier's "carbon neutral" claim makes it your claim. "Our vendor certified it" is not a substantiation.
